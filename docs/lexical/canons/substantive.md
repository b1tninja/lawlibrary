# Substantive canons

Substantive canons are background rules about what the law is presumed to do when the text leaves a choice open. They are not grammar. A lexical engine does not apply them; it flags the words and structures that make a canon relevant so a later reader can.

Illustrations below are labeled as such. They are not holdings and are not quotations.

## 1. Constitutional-avoidance canon

If a reading of a statute would raise a serious constitutional doubt, prefer a fair alternative reading that does not.

The soft form prefers the reading that avoids the doubt. The hard form refuses a reading that would make the statute unconstitutional when another reading is available. For the engine: marks of doubt are vague verbs of power ("regulate", "necessary", "appropriate") paired with a rights or structure trigger elsewhere in the clause set.

## 2. Rule of lenity

Ambiguity in a criminal statute is resolved against the government and in favor of the defendant.

Lenity is a last resort after ordinary tools leave a genuine tie. It covers the elements of the offense and the authorized punishment. Illustration: if "knowingly" can attach to one element or to all of them, and both attachments are grammatically open, lenity favors the narrower attachment.

## 3. Presumption against retroactivity

Statutes are presumed to operate prospectively unless Congress has made a clear contrary command.

Landgraf v. USI Film Products states the modern framework: new liability for completed conduct needs a clear statement; procedural and prospective effects may attach without one. For the engine: watch effective-date clauses, "shall apply to", "pending", "before the date of enactment", and definitions of covered "acts" or "conduct".

## 4. Presumption against extraterritoriality

Federal statutes are presumed not to apply outside the United States unless Congress clearly says they do.

Morrison v. National Australia Bank and later cases ask whether the statute's focus occurred in the United States. For the engine: "foreign", "anywhere", "in commerce", place nouns, and jurisdiction headings are the signals; bare global verbs are not enough.

## 5. Federalism clear-statement rule

Congress does not alter the federal-state balance, commandeer state officers, or displace traditional state domains unless it speaks with unusual clarity.

Gregory v. Ashcroft is a leading modern statement of the idea. For the engine: words that touch states, officers, immunity, preemption, and "notwithstanding state law" need a clear-statement pass before a broad reading sticks.

## 6. Presumption against waiver of sovereign immunity

A waiver of sovereign immunity is strictly construed and must be clear; ambiguity is resolved against the waiver.

The rule covers who may be sued, for what relief, and in which forum. Illustration: "sue and be sued" may open a door; it does not by itself invent damages or strip every defense unless the text does that work.

## 7. Presumption of mens rea for a criminal offense

A criminal offense is presumed to require a culpable mental state for each material element unless Congress clearly dispenses with it.

Morissette v. United States is the leading modern restatement. Strict-liability readings are disfavored for traditional common-law wrongs and for elements that separate innocent from wrongful conduct. For the engine: "knowingly", "willfully", "intentionally", "with intent to", and silence where those words appear on sibling offenses are the marks.

## 8. Chevron deference (overruled)

Under Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc., a court first asked whether Congress had spoken to the precise question and, if not, deferred to a reasonable agency reading of the ambiguity.

Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo (2024) overruled that framework. Courts must decide questions of law by independent judgment; ambiguity alone does not hand the meaning of the statute to the agency. Prior holdings that rested on Chevron remain entitled to statutory stare decisis as holdings, but Chevron is not the rule going forward. For the engine: treat "defer", "permissible construction", and "Chevron step two" in secondary text as historical labels, not live instructions.

## 9. Skidmore respect

Under Skidmore v. Swift & Co., an agency's interpretation may persuade a court to the extent of its thoroughness, logic, consistency, and other marks of care, without controlling the outcome.

## 10. Presumption against implied repeal

Later statutes are not read to repeal earlier ones by implication; repeal requires a clear statement or an irreconcilable conflict.

Courts prefer a reading that lets both enactments stand. For the engine: "notwithstanding any other provision", "supersedes", "repealed", and cross-references to named acts are express signals; bare overlap of subject matter is not.

## 11. Statutes in derogation of the common law are strictly construed

A statute that cuts against the common law is read narrowly, and only the displacement the text requires is taken.

The canon is older than modern textualism and is often softened when the statute is a comprehensive code. Illustration: a statute that names one common-law defense as removed does not erase every other defense unless the words do that work.

## 12. Remedial statutes are liberally construed

Statutes enacted to remedy a wrong or expand a remedy are often said to be read broadly to advance their purpose.

Critics argue that this fights the textual canons: once purpose drives breadth, ordinary meaning, expressio unius, and similar constraints lose their bite. A lexical engine should record the "remedial" label when the statute or its title claims one, and should not treat the label as a license to ignore operative verbs and limits.

## 13. Charming Betsy canon

Murray v. The Charming Betsy stands for the proposition that an ambiguous statute should be read, where fairly possible, not to violate international law.

## 14. Indian canon

Ambiguity in a statute enacted for the benefit of Indian tribes is construed in the tribes' favor, and treaties and related texts are read generously toward tribal rights.

The canon is at its strongest for treaties and for statutes that implement trust or treaty promises. It is weaker when the statute is a general regulatory measure that only happens to affect tribes.

## 15. Veterans canon

The veterans canon—often traced to Brown v. Gardner—is a recognized substantive canon: interpretive doubt in veterans' benefits statutes is resolved in the veteran's favor.

It is a tiebreaker after ordinary tools, not a free rewrite of clear text. Recent Supreme Court opinions have applied it sparingly and some Justices have questioned how much weight it should carry; treat it as live but contested, and do not invent a stronger form than "doubt favors the veteran."

## 16. Tax statutes

The traditional canon, associated with Gould v. Gould, is that statutes levying taxes are not extended by implication and, in case of doubt, are construed against the government and in favor of the taxpayer.

A long-running counter-canon treats deductions, exemptions, exclusions, and credits as matters of legislative grace, construed narrowly against the taxpayer. How far either rule still moves outcomes under modern textualism is debated; if the text is clear, neither canon adds a thumb on the scale. Do not invent a single unified "tax canon" beyond those two strands.

## 17. Absurdity doctrine

A reading that ordinary tools would otherwise support may be rejected when it produces a result no reasonable legislature could have meant, if another fair reading exists.

Absurdity is narrow. Odd policy, harsh results, or a drafting inelegance is not enough. Illustration: a cross-reference that, read literally, erases the only section that gives the cross-reference meaning may be a candidate; a costly but coherent compliance duty usually is not.

## 18. Clear-statement rules as a family

Clear-statement rules are a family of substantive canons that refuse a strong legal effect unless Congress used unmistakably clear words.

Effects that typically demand clarity include retroactivity, extraterritoriality, abrogation of state or tribal sovereign immunity, displacement of the federal-state balance, implied repeal, and waiver of generally available judicial review. For a lexer, the payoff words are "clearly", "expressly", "explicitly", "specifically", "notwithstanding", and "unless"; also watch "only if", "except as provided", and named-act repeals. Absence of those markers does not decide the case, but presence is what the engine should surface for a clear-statement pass.

## 19. Presumption of judicial review

Agency action and other administrative determinations are presumed subject to judicial review; preclusion of review requires a clear statement or a convincing alternative scheme.

The presumption is especially strong for constitutional claims and for questions of law. For the engine: "final", "not subject to review", "committed to agency discretion", channeling clauses, and exclusive-jurisdiction grants are the marks that can rebut it.

## 20. Elephants in mouseholes

Congress does not alter a fundamental detail of a regulatory scheme in vague or ancillary terms.

Whitman v. American Trucking Assns. is the usual name for that one-sentence idea.
